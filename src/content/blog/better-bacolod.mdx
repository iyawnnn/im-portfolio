Building something in your own repository is different from contributing to someone else’s. In your own project, you usually remember why a component has its name, where a value comes from, and which shortcuts you took. In an unfamiliar codebase, that context is not guaranteed. BetterBacolod was my first real open-source contribution, so I was learning how to work inside software that other people already maintained.

I found the [BetterGov PH](https://bettergov.ph/) civic-tech projects while looking for a place to contribute, and that led me to the [BetterBacolod repository](https://github.com/betterbacolod/betterbacolod). It is an existing open-source project focused on making Bacolod public data easier to explore. Its code already had component patterns, data shapes, product decisions, and people maintaining it. My first task was to understand enough of the system to make a change that fit.

### Read before you rewrite

I started by following how the existing dashboards connected their data, tables, and charts. A visualization was not just a chart component. It depended on how values were shaped, how filters changed the displayed records, and what users were meant to understand from the result. The same was true of navigation and shared interface elements: a small change could affect more than the screen where I first noticed it.

That meant reading surrounding code before deciding what needed to change. I had to distinguish a bug from a behavior that was intentional, and a local improvement from a change that would make the rest of the project harder to follow. Existing patterns were useful evidence about what maintainers were trying to preserve, even when I would have made a different choice in a project of my own.

A contribution can be technically correct and still be a poor fit if it ignores the project around it. Understanding that took more thought than opening a file and starting to edit.

### Small fixes carry responsibility

One example was a budget chart with a fixed upper limit. That made the visualization depend on a particular range of values. As the dataset changed, the chart could become less useful. I adjusted the scale logic to respond to the data instead of relying on that one fixed maximum.

The change was narrow, but it still needed to fit the chart and the data already used by the dashboard. I needed to understand what the numbers represented and how the surrounding view handled filters before changing the calculation. The goal was not to replace the visualization or redesign the page; it was to make the existing behavior less brittle.

I also contributed to accessibility in interface components and worked with historical data that needed correction. Those changes had different shapes, but the same responsibility applied: understand what the current interface promised, make a focused adjustment, and check that the result still made sense in context.

Open-source work often looks like a small correction from the outside. Inside the repository, it carries the responsibility to avoid breaking behavior people already rely on and to make the reason for the change clear to whoever reviews it.

### Writing code other people have to live with

In a solo project, it is easy to write code that only makes sense to you. You remember what a short name means and why a helper has an unusual shape. In a shared repository, another person has to review the change, and someone else may need to maintain it later.

That changed what I considered a good improvement. A broad rewrite can look cleaner to its author but make review harder and introduce more risk than the original issue requires. A smaller change that follows the project’s conventions is often easier to understand, discuss, and maintain.

I had to ask whether a change solved the issue at hand, whether its naming and structure fit nearby code, and whether I was adding complexity because the project needed it. The goal was not to leave my style on the repository. It was to make a useful change that belonged there.

That did not mean copying every pattern uncritically. Understanding the context let me explain when an existing assumption needed to change.

### Becoming a maintainer

BetterBacolod was my first open-source contribution, so I did not expect it to become more than a few focused changes. After my contributions and pull requests were reviewed, [Matt Enarle](https://mattenarle.com/) invited me to continue as a maintainer. I appreciated that trust. What began as a few improvements became an ongoing responsibility to consider the project beyond my own code: what should be merged, whether the next contributor will understand it, and whether an improvement is worth the churn.

The BetterBacolod team also shared a [video highlighting the dashboard updates](https://www.facebook.com/reel/1496372124793253), which was meaningful recognition for my first contribution. I’m grateful to Matt and the team for the opportunity to contribute and continue as a maintainer.

The experience taught me that contributing well is less about making a repository look like yours and more about understanding it well enough to make one useful change belong there.
